I wonder what expression I might wear if, near the end of my life, I still remember that hamster. Even now, I cannot quite say what it left behind in my life.

I boarded at school for a few years during primary school. Schoolwork was not yet particularly demanding, and there were always long stretches of time to fill after class. My roommate acquired a hamster from somewhere, kept it in a little box in our dormitory, and named it Little Coalball.

Little Coalball had a bad temper, or at least that is how I remember it. My roommate liked to hold it in his hands and play with it, and was bitten every few days, sometimes hard enough to break the skin. Naturally, I kept my distance. In those years, I spent most afternoons playing football on the school field, often returning to the dormitory only as the sky began to darken. Most of the time, Little Coalball was simply a rustling presence in a corner of the room. Occasionally, when my roommate was away, I would top up its food and water and peer into the box to see what it was up to.

I probably never imagined then that I would have much to do with this hamster.

After primary school, neither my roommate nor I chose to stay on in the school's junior secondary division, so we both had to move out of the dormitory. He left in a hurry, with plenty of luggage, and there was already a cat at his home. In the end, he did not take Little Coalball with him. I can no longer remember why the job fell to me. Perhaps he asked me casually; perhaps I thought it would hardly be any trouble. In any case, by the time I realized it, Little Coalball had become my responsibility.

And so, having never kept a small animal before, I found myself pressed into service as its keeper.

The first few days were rather chaotic. I had no idea how much a hamster should eat in a day, or how often I ought to change the bedding it kept pushing around its box. I even went to ask a friend who had a dog. Looking back, asking a dog owner how to care for a hamster seems a little like seeking medical advice from anyone who happens to be nearby. At the time, though, I did not find it funny. I was simply afraid that one careless mistake might make the little creature my roommate had entrusted to me ill.

Once the summer holiday officially began, Little Coalball stayed in the school dormitory for a while. Every few days, I had to cycle over to refill its water, add some food, and check that nothing in the box was amiss.

Summers in Nanjing are very hot. At noon, the sunlight on the road was so dazzling that I could barely keep my eyes open. By the time I reached school, the back of my shirt was often soaked with sweat. The empty campus felt quite unlike its usual self. There were no noisy voices in the corridors, and Little Coalball was the dormitory's only remaining resident.

It clearly had no idea how much trouble I had gone to in coming all that way. Usually, when I opened the box, it would trot over, sniff my fingers, then turn quite casually to rummaging through the food I had just put in. Sometimes I tried to pick it up and play with it for a while, but it showed no appreciation. The moment I let my guard down, it would reward me with another bite.

I would often call it ungrateful while checking whether it had broken the skin, only to find myself looking back before leaving to make sure its water bowl was sitting securely.

Later, when my time in the dormitory was up, I simply brought the box and running wheel home.

That spared me the bicycle journeys, though Little Coalball went from being a lodger I visited every few days to a housemate I had to deal with daily.

Gradually, I learned some of its habits. It liked to carry food into a corner and hide it away. Even with plenty left in the bowl, it always seemed worried about where its next meal would come from. During the day, it sometimes refused to stir no matter how I tried to coax it, yet at night it could run tirelessly on its wheel for ages. In the quiet of the night, I would occasionally hear the faint sound of the wheel turning. At first it irritated me, but I grew used to it and eventually thought little of it.

Whether Little Coalball was growing up or simply preferred my home to the school dormitory, its temper seemed to have improved considerably. Sometimes, when I reached into the box, it would come over to sniff my hand and even stay still for a little while. This did not, of course, mean that it had stopped biting. It would still nip me without warning whenever I relaxed, as though determined to remind me that we were not quite that close.

I did complain. Hamster food cost money, the box needed cleaning, and before going out to play I had to remember to check that it had water. Sometimes, just when I was enjoying myself outside, I would remember that I had not fed Little Coalball and reluctantly head home. Back then, I always felt I had been taken hostage by a hamster. I had finally reached a summer with no homework, only to find myself saddled with another chore.

Looking back now, though, those chores do not seem quite so tiresome.

I no longer remember what I watched on television that summer, how many games of football I played, or even how I filled each day. Strangely, I can still picture myself crouching beside the box in the early evening, watching Little Coalball hold a piece of food in its front paws and nibble away. Sometimes I did nothing at all, just sat beside it and watched it go about its business. A surprising amount of time could pass that way.

There were few momentous events to record that summer. Day after day, I gave Little Coalball water, fed it, and changed its bedding. It went on eating, sleeping, running on its wheel, and occasionally biting me. We each lived our own lives, and without noticing it, became part of each other's.

Although I had cared for Little Coalball for two or three months, I had never really prepared myself to keep a hamster for the long term. Perhaps it was because I had only meant to help my roommate out. Perhaps I had not yet thought about what would happen once school started. Whatever the reason, summer went by, and I never seriously considered where Little Coalball would go next.

Near the end of the holiday, I took Little Coalball with me on a visit to my family's hometown.

My younger cousin seemed very fond of it. He would come over every few days, sometimes staying for half the day. He was less afraid of being bitten than I was and always wanted to pick Little Coalball up and play with it. I found his enthusiasm a little amusing. To my mind, there was only so much amusement to be had from a hamster that did little besides eat, sleep, run on its wheel, and occasionally bite someone.

When my aunt and uncle noticed, they tentatively asked whether I might leave Little Coalball with my cousin.

It seemed a good idea. My cousin liked it, his parents were willing to help look after it, and I probably would not have as much time once school began. So I agreed.

I cannot remember whether I told him how to feed it, how often to change its water, or how to clean the box. Even if I did, I imagine it was no more than a few hurried instructions before I left. After all, I had only recently figured these things out myself.

And that was how Little Coalball stayed behind in my family's hometown.

Then I started junior secondary school. As the workload grew heavier, I rarely asked after Little Coalball. Occasionally, a relative would mention it, and I would hear that it was still at my cousin's house. I did not give it much thought.

Some time later, I happened to be talking with my great-aunt back home about my cousin, and somehow Little Coalball came up. She said he was not particularly attentive to the hamster and did not take much care of it. But she also mentioned that Little Coalball's temper seemed much better than before. It hardly bit anyone anymore.

I do not think I said much at the time.

Only later, when her words occasionally came back to me, would I find myself wondering what exactly she had meant by not taking much care of it.

Perhaps my cousin was simply young and sometimes forgot to feed it, and his parents naturally stepped in. Or perhaps Little Coalball's life afterwards really was not very good. I do not know. As for why it had stopped biting, I have even less basis for guessing.

Sometimes, remembering how I left Little Coalball behind, I realize that I never seem to have seriously considered whether my cousin knew how to care for a hamster. He liked it, his parents had agreed, and that had seemed enough. Then school grew busy, and gradually I forgot even to ask how it was doing.

It would be hard to say there was anything obviously wrong with my decision. My cousin really did like Little Coalball, and I could not have known what would happen afterwards. Even if I had brought it home, I might not have cared for it any better once I was in junior secondary school.

Yet none of this reasoning seems quite enough to settle my doubts.

Many years have passed now. When I look back on that summer, the fingers Little Coalball bit have long since stopped hurting. But the time I spent caring for it has become one of the few stretches of memory not buried beneath Jiangsu exam papers.

I still remember the empty school dormitory during the holidays, the road I cycled along that summer, and the way Little Coalball would stuff food into its mouth and scurry about with bulging cheeks. Sometimes, when I reached into the box, it would come over to sniff my hand, then bite me a little later without warning.

What became of all these things afterwards, I do not know.

I do not know whether my cousin took good care of it, how much longer it lived back home, or whether, at the end, it was still living in the box I had brought it in.

None of this would have been difficult to find out. Someone among my relatives would still remember. A casual question might have told me roughly what happened.

But all these years later, I have never asked.
